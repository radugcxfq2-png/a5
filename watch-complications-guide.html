The Complications Guide

A complication is any job a watch does beyond showing the time. Some are useful every day, some are pure pleasure, and each has one or two rules for setting it safely.

01Date and day-date

The most common complication, and the most used. A disc with the numbers 1 to 31 turns once a day beneath the dial. Because every month is treated as 31 days long, you correct it by hand five times a year, after each shorter month.

Most watches have a quick-set position: pull the crown out one click and turn to advance the date. On a mechanical watch, avoid doing this between about nine in the evening and three in the morning. The gears that change the date are engaged during those hours and can be damaged if forced.

Safe habit: move the hands to half past six, set the date to yesterday, then wind the hands forward past midnight to today.

02Chronograph

A chronograph is a stopwatch that shares a case with the watch. The long central seconds hand belongs to it and normally rests at twelve. One or two small sub-dials count elapsed minutes and sometimes hours, and another shows the running seconds of the time itself.

The top pusher starts and stops. The bottom pusher resets, and should be pressed only when the chronograph is stopped. Do not operate the pushers under water unless the manual says you may.

03Tachymeter

The numbers around the bezel of many chronographs form a tachymeter. It answers one question: at this pace, how many units per hour? Start the chronograph at a marker, stop it after exactly one kilometre or one mile, and the seconds hand points to your speed.

04GMT and second time zone

A GMT watch adds a fourth hand that goes round once in 24 hours, read against a 24-hour scale on the bezel or dial. Because it cannot confuse morning with evening, it is ideal for tracking a place far away.

There are two kinds. On a traveller’s GMT, the ordinary hour hand jumps in one-hour steps, so you change local time on arrival and leave the 24-hour hand on home time. On a caller’s GMT, the 24-hour hand is the one that adjusts, which suits people who stay put and want to follow another city.

05Moonphase

A disc painted with two moons turns behind a shaped window, showing the moon as it appears in the sky. A full cycle takes about 29 and a half days. Standard mechanisms round this slightly and drift by a day roughly every two and a half years.

To set it, advance the disc until the moon is full in the centre of the window, then move it forward one step for each day since the last full moon. Like the date, do not adjust it in the late evening hours.

06Power reserve indicator

A small gauge, like a fuel needle, showing how many hours the mainspring has left. It is most useful on hand-wound watches, where it tells you when to wind, and on watches with long reserves that are worn in rotation.

07Choosing what you will use

Every complication adds parts, thickness and something else to reset when the watch has stopped. Be honest about your days. A frequent traveller will use a GMT constantly. A cook will use a chronograph or a rotating bezel. Almost everyone uses a date. Choose the functions you will reach for, and let the rest remain things to admire on other people’s wrists.
